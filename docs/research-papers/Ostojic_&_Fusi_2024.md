## Abstract
One major challenge of neuroscience is identifying structure in seemingly disorganized neural activity. Different types of structure have different computational implications that help this can help neuroscientists understand the functional role of a particular brain area. Here we outline a unified approach to characterize structure by inspecting the representational geometry and the modularity properties of the recorded activity and show that a similar approach can also reveal structure in connectivity. We start by setting up a general framework for determining geometry and modularity in activity and connectivity and relating these properties with computations performed by the network. Network. We then use this framework to review the types of structure found in recent studies of model networks performing three classes of computations.
## Glossary Learning
- **Abstract representation**: a neural representation that depends only on one variable when projected in a linear subspace. Other variables are represented in approximately orthogonal subspaces, allowing a simple linear decoder to generalize across their values.
- **Aligned representations**: neural representations where different groups of neurons encode different task variables, and hence the coding directions of different task variables are aligned to the neural axes.
- **Disentangled/factorized representation**: neural representations taht encode information about two or more task variables along orthogonal directions so that the representation of one variable is invariant with respect to variations of the other variables.
- **Generalization**:Capability to infer correct responses in novel situations, for example, when presented with previously unseen stimuli.
- **Latent task variables**； Low dimensional parameters that generate the space of stimulus inputs explored in the task.
- **Latent variables**: Sources of shared variability across observed neural activities. They can be divided into two types. One, external latent variables, which denote shared variability induced by sensory stimuli. Two, internal latent variables, which describe shared variability independent of stimuli, that is, generated by intrinsic dynamics.
- **Neural computations**：Progressive transformation of sensory inputs into behavior outputs through a series of neural representations. Examples include dimensionality expansion, feature extraction, or feature selection.
- **Neural representations**: Mapping from the set of inputs to patterns of neural responses, recorded in a brain area or generated in a network model.
- **Selectivity of a neuron**: Statistical quantification (example, use a linear regression model) of the response of a neuron to give a given stimulus or  task variable also denoted as tuning.
- **Selectivity space**: space where each axis represents the selectivity with respect to one task variable, and each point represents the selectivity of one neuron with respect to all task variables.
### How to get a understanding of latent variables?
>Latent variables: sources of shared variability across observed neural activities. They can be divided into two types: (i) external latent variables, which denote shared variability induced by sensory stimuli; (ii) internal latent variables, which describe shared variability independent of stimuli, that is, generated by intrinsic dynamics.

- Latent variables in neuroscience represent hidden patterns of coordination among neurons, distinguishing between those driven by the outside world (external) and those generated by the brain's own internal activity (internal).

- **The Concept of Latent Variables**
    - In neuroscience, we often record the activity of hundreds or thousands of neurons simultaneously. However, neurons do not act in total isolation; they are parts of a network. A **latent variable** is a mathematical way of describing a "hidden" signal that influences many neurons at once. You cannot see the latent variable directly in the raw data, but you can see its effects because it causes multiple neurons to fire in a synchronized or correlated way. Think of it like watching a group of dancers: you see the individual movements (observed activity), but the music they are all listening to is the "latent variable" that coordinates their steps.

- **External Latent Variables: The Sensory Input**
    - The first type, **external latent variables**, refers to shared patterns of activity that are forced upon the brain by the environment. When you look at a picture or hear a sound, that specific sensory stimulus sends a wave of information through your nervous system. Because many neurons are processing the same input, they will show "shared variability." In this case, the "music" the neurons are dancing to is coming from the outside world. If the stimulus changes, these external latent variables change accordingly.

- **Internal Latent Variables: The Brain's Own Voice**
    - The second type, **internal latent variables**, describes activity that happens "under the hood," independent of what you are currently seeing or hearing. Even in a dark, quiet room, your brain is highly active. This activity is generated by **intrinsic dynamics**—the brain’s internal wiring, ongoing thoughts, attention levels, or physiological states like hunger or arousal. These variables represent the brain "talking to itself." They explain why, even when presented with the exact same stimulus twice, your neural response might be different the second time; your internal state has shifted.

- **Why This Distinction Matters**
    - Separating these two types of variables allows researchers to understand how much of our brain activity is a direct reflection of the world (external) versus how much is a product of our own internal processing, expectations, and biological state (internal). It helps in mapping out how the brain balances "bottom-up" information from the senses with "top-down" control from internal cognitive processes.
